<h3>Trade Liberalization and Firm Adjustment: Evidence from Pakistan</h3>
<p class="authors">With Zehra Farooq (FBR, Pakistan)</p>
<p class="paper-meta">Draft: October 2026 · <a href="{{ '/files/Umer_Sohail_JMP.pdf' | relative_url }}">Paper (PDF)</a></p>
<details open><summary>Abstract</summary><p>We study how firms adjust to preferential access to a large export market. Between November 2012 and January 2014, the European Union removed most of its remaining tariffs on Pakistani products in two steps—flood-relief preferences on a narrow set of products, followed by GSP+—without requiring tariff cuts in return. Using corporate tax records for 3,810 firms and variation across products in tariffs before the cuts, we find that each percentage point of tariff exposure raised firm export value by about 19 percent, mainly after the second, broader step, and Pakistan's exports shifted toward the EU. Export growth came with lower recorded production wage bills, while the evidence on capital is inconclusive. Export estimates are larger for more productive firms, and less productive firms became less likely to report positive sales or wages; among large firms, exporters expanded exports and cut production wage bills, while some non-exporters increased their recorded capital stock.</p></details>

<h3>Causal Impact of Barter Trade Policy during a Foreign Exchange Reserves Crisis</h3>
<p class="authors">With Fraz Ahmed (Carnegie Mellon University)</p>
<p class="paper-meta">Draft: June 2026 · <a href="{{ '/files/barter_pak_afg.pdf' | relative_url }}">Paper (PDF)</a></p>
<details open><summary>Abstract</summary><p>When Pakistan’s foreign exchange reserves collapsed in 2023, the government introduced a barter trade mechanism allowing direct goods-for-goods exchange with Afghanistan, bypassing frozen banking channels. Using transaction-level customs data and product-level variation in barter eligibility, we provide causal evidence that alternative payment mechanisms can mitigate crisis-induced trade disruptions. Barter-eligible products experienced 30% higher export quantities during the crisis peak relative to ineligible products. These effects were crisis-contingent, emerging during reserve depletion and dissipating as conventional payment systems resumed. The mechanism operated through real trade expansion with minimal price distortion, demonstrating that well-designed temporary interventions can sustain trade during payment system failures without generating persistent distortions.</p></details>
</article>

<h3>Towards Win-Win: Evidence from Commercial Dispute Resolution in India</h3>
<p class="authors">With Manaswini Rao (University of Delaware), Sandhya Seetharaman, and Raghav Pandey</p>
<p class="paper-meta">Draft: September 2026 · <a href="{{ '/files/Commercial_Courts_wlegalanalysis.pdf' | relative_url }}">Paper (PDF)</a></p>
<details open><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Ineffective courts can delay economic development when legal disputes remain unresolved. This is particularly concerning for India where over 50 million suits are pending in courts for more than a year - a statistic that hasn’t changed in over a decade. Using random case assignment to judges and policies on mediation, we provide novel causal evidence on the economic benefits of mediation as a dispute resolution strategy for contract disputes in India. Assignment to a “settlement-prone” judge increases dispute resolution by 18 percentage points. Consequently, plaintiff firms’ profits increase by 18%, a persistent effect. Conversely, defendant firms’ profits decrease by 30% around the settlement timing, although this effect attenuates over time. Reduced uncertainty from dispute resolution, particularly for firms with fewer cases, is a plausible mechanism driving these results.</p></details>
</article>
