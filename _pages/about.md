I am a fourth-year Ph.D. student in Economics at the University of Delaware, specializing in applied microeconomics with a focus on international trade, development economics, and firm behavior.

My research uses large-scale microdata and causal inference methods to study how policy environments shape firm-level outcomes in developing economies. One strand of my work examines how trade liberalization affects manufacturing firms in Pakistan—analyzing the impact of tariff reforms on firm productivity, market dynamics, and employment. A second strand studies barter trade as a crisis mitigation tool: when Pakistan's foreign exchange reserves collapsed in 2023, the government introduced a barter mechanism allowing direct goods-for-goods exchange with Afghanistan, bypassing frozen banking channels. Using transaction-level customs data and product-level variation in barter eligibility, I provide causal evidence that barter-eligible products experienced 30% higher export quantities during the crisis peak, and that these well-designed temporary interventions can sustain trade during payment system failures without generating persistent distortions. A third strand investigates how judicial institutions and contract enforcement influence firm performance in India, combining administrative firm data with detailed court records to quantify the economic costs of legal frictions.

Methodologically, I work extensively with large administrative and survey datasets, applying techniques such as difference-in-differences, instrumental variables, and event study designs to extract causal insights from observational data. I am proficient in Stata, R, and Python, and have experience building data pipelines for cleaning, merging, and analyzing complex datasets.

My broader research agenda sits at the intersection of globalization, institutional quality, and economic development—questions that are central to both academic inquiry and evidence-based policy and business strategy.
